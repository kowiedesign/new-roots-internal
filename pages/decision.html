Decision Document

Sapana Restaurant Acquisition

Version 0.6 · Last updated 2026-07-28 · Not a substitute for professional legal, tax, immigration, or accounting advice
The question this document answers: would we still be glad we made this decision ten years from now, even if things don't go according to plan? That means judging Sapana on lifestyle, immigration, and community outcomes — not financial return alone.

Executive Summary

Acquire Sapana, an operating restaurant in Yamanashi, as the founding business of New Roots Collective — a company built for the long term around stewarding, and eventually starting, businesses that strengthen this rural community.

The current owner isn't exiting. He wants to stay on and run the restaurant day to day, moving from owner to employee, primarily to find a sustainable visa path. That gives us an operating business with existing revenue, an operator who wants to stay, and a visa outcome for him that's a genuine good on its own. We also have a trusted local advisor in Minobu already invested in this succeeding.

What excites us

  • An operating, revenue-generating business rather than a startup
  • A cooperative operator staying on, easing our own operational learning curve
  • A concrete first step toward New Roots Collective's broader vision, with a real local relationship already in place
  • A visa outcome for the owner, and potentially other staff, that stands on its own as a good outcome

What could go wrong

  • Financials could be weaker than hoped once verified — see Finances
  • Neither of us has restaurant or hospitality operating experience
  • The immigration path could prove harder than expected once a specialist reviews it — see Immigration
  • Rural Yamanashi's demographic decline is a structural headwind independent of this restaurant
  • Local trust takes time to earn, and we start as outsiders — see Local Trust, below
Current read: worth pursuing further. The next steps are about confirming financials and immigration guidance — not about whether the underlying idea is sound.

Background

Why this matters to us

We've become close with our neighbors here — invited into homes, included in local events, trusted in ways that take years to earn anywhere. People who've lived here longer describe how vibrant and prosperous this town used to be, and how much has quietly been lost as it aged and emptied out.

We're not trying to "save" the town — that framing puts us above something we're still learning to be part of. We want to become part of its next chapter: put down roots here ourselves, and help the businesses and families already here put down deeper roots too.

What "New Roots" means

  • Putting down our own roots in this community
  • Helping existing businesses develop new roots, rather than replacing what's already growing
  • Encouraging new families and entrepreneurs to settle and take root here
  • Building new ideas that stay rooted in local tradition, not imported wholesale
  • Continuing and nurturing what exists rather than starting over
  • Businesses that grow from shared roots, strengthening each other rather than competing
  • Investments made for the health of the roots, not just the harvest — for the generations after us
  • Roots as the foundation new growth grows from — the deeper and wider the roots, the more stable and long-lasting the forest above can be

The opportunity, in brief

  • The owner currently holds a Business Manager visa
  • He appears unlikely to qualify for renewal under the current rules without a change in circumstances
  • He's exploring ways to continue living and working in Japan long-term
  • One possible solution: restructure the restaurant's ownership, with him staying on as an employee rather than owner

The current shape of the deal: we acquire the business, take on its financial, administrative, and legal burden, and he stays on as operator — customers should see little or no visible change. Full detail: Immigration, Finances.

Owner motivation

What excites us

His family's future in Japan likely depends on a stable outcome here, so his success and ours are genuinely aligned. We intend to leave day-to-day control largely in his hands.

What gives us pause

Wanting this to succeed isn't the same as having the capacity to make it succeed. Someone under real visa pressure may understandably present the business more favorably than it is, or agree to terms he later struggles to honor — a reason for us to be more careful and fair in diligence and terms, not less.

Community support

We have a relationship with a respected local business owner in Minobu who has spent years successfully revitalizing the area and has deep local relationships. She's invested in Sapana's success and has offered introductions and guidance — an advantage most outside acquirers wouldn't have.

Local Trust

Trust in a small rural community is earned over years, not granted on arrival. To some people, our first business may initially look like foreigners buying another foreign-owned business, regardless of intentions. That perception won't disappear just because we mean well or already have good relationships.

  • Actions matter far more than announcements or good intentions
  • Preserving what people already love about the restaurant, and respecting local norms, will do more for trust than anything we say
  • Our existing relationships and Minobu's support reduce this challenge — they don't remove it

This isn't a reason to hesitate. It's a reason to expect that earning full local trust takes time regardless of how the deal goes, and to plan for that patiently.

Long-Term Opportunity

If Sapana works, it's a plausible first step toward something larger:

  • A track record and local trust that make the next acquisition easier than the first
  • A model for helping other retiring or struggling local owners — easing a succession or a role transition rather than watching a business close
  • Access to inexpensive rural real estate and unused buildings as a platform for renovation and new uses
  • Shared operational infrastructure — accounting, admin, property management, financing — built once and reused, so specialists (a chef, a shopkeeper, a farmer) can focus on their craft
  • A gradually assembled portfolio of complementary businesses that reinforce each other and the community

Success even if Sapana is the only business we ever own

Expansion is upside, not the bar for success. Sapana alone is a complete, worthwhile outcome if:

  • The restaurant becomes financially healthy
  • The owner and staff are able to remain in Japan
  • We genuinely enjoy operating it
  • It contributes positively to the community
This decision needs to stand on its own merits, even in a future where Sapana is the only business we ever own.

Long-Term Flexibility

Owning a Japanese company changes our own future optionality in ways worth understanding, not resolving today:

  • Does it increase or reduce our flexibility to change plans later?
  • Does it make it harder to move away from Japan if circumstances change?
  • Does it affect either of our paths to Permanent Residency? See current PR policy changes.
  • Does it create ongoing obligations that continue even if we stop actively operating businesses?
  • Could the company go dormant if priorities shift, without major cost or complication?

None of these need answers before the August 6 meeting. Full detail and the current PR policy tracker: Immigration.

Decision Checklist

  • Ten years from now, we'd likely be glad we made this decision — even if it doesn't go according to plan
  • We've reviewed real financials and understand true adjusted profitability
  • We've chosen an acquisition structure with a clear reason why
  • We have written, current immigration guidance confirming our structure solves the owner's visa situation
  • We understand the lease and material liabilities
  • We've agreed on a fair purchase price directly with the owner — not assumed
  • We've consulted an accountant, lawyer, and immigration specialist
  • We're both aligned on proceeding, understanding the risks, not just the vision
