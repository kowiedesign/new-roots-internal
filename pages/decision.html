What's working in our favor, and why it matters

  • Existing customers and revenue mean we're not starting from zero — cash flow begins on day one instead of after months of ramp-up, and a proven operating model gives us a stronger foundation than building something new
  • The owner staying on lowers transition risk by keeping the person who already knows how to run the place, and his credible path off Business Manager status means New Roots Collective likely won't need to raise ¥30M in capital to make this work
  • Neither founder has to change their current visa status to take this on, which means no immigration risk to Corey's Indeed employment or May's existing status just from proceeding
  • Existing trust with neighbors and the Minobu advisor means we're not starting community relationships from scratch — a real head start most outside acquirers wouldn't have

What's working against us, and why it matters

  • Because the financials are still unverified, we don't yet know our real risk exposure — we could be underestimating how much capital or time this actually requires
  • If the recommended immigration path doesn't hold up under professional review, the deal structure and cost could change materially — this is the single biggest point of failure right now
  • Without hospitality experience, we should expect a steeper learning curve, a higher chance of avoidable operational mistakes, and a real need to lean on experienced partners while we build our own knowledge
  • Being newcomers means some community trust still has to be earned no matter how well this deal goes — that's expected, not a sign something's wrong

What could change the recommendation

  • Verified financials showing losses meaningfully worse than the ~¥1M/month reconsideration trigger
  • An immigration specialist concluding the Skilled Labor path doesn't fit, and that Business Manager-level capital is required after all
  • Discovery of major undisclosed liabilities or an unfavorable, non-assignable lease

Background

Why this matters to us

We're drawn to this town's story — how vibrant and prosperous it used to be, and how much has quietly been lost as it aged and emptied out. We're not trying to "save" it; that framing puts us above something we're still learning to be part of. We want to become part of its next chapter: put down roots here ourselves, and help the businesses and families already here put down deeper roots too.

What "New Roots" means

The name reflects our approach: we steward and strengthen what already exists — this restaurant, this community — rather than replacing it, and we invest for the health of the roots, not just the next harvest. If it works, the same approach can help other local businesses and welcome new families into the area, one relationship at a time rather than one announcement at a time.

The opportunity, in brief

  • The owner currently holds a Business Manager visa
  • He appears unlikely to qualify for renewal under the current rules without a change in circumstances
  • He's exploring ways to continue living and working in Japan long-term
  • One possible solution: restructure the restaurant's ownership, with him staying on as an employee rather than owner

The current shape of the deal: we acquire the business, take on its financial, administrative, and legal burden, and he stays on as operator — customers should see little or no visible change. His success and ours are genuinely aligned here, though that alignment is a reason for us to be careful and fair in diligence and terms, not a reason to skip them. Community Support

We've become close with our neighbors, many of whom have lived in this area for generations. They've welcomed us, are supportive of us putting down roots locally, and are genuinely excited about the possibility of younger people investing in the town. They also have extensive relationships throughout the community — both local residents and official channels — that we don't yet have on our own.

Separately, we have a relationship with a respected local business owner in Minobu who has spent years successfully revitalizing the area and has deep relationships of her own. She's invested in Sapana's success and has offered introductions and guidance.

Together, these are a meaningful advantage — but not a substitute for the work of earning trust ourselves over time (see Local Trust, below).

Long-Term Opportunity

If Sapana works, it's a plausible first step toward something larger:

  • A track record and local trust that make the next acquisition easier than the first
  • A model for helping other retiring or struggling local owners — easing a succession or a role transition rather than watching a business close
  • Access to inexpensive rural real estate and unused buildings as a platform for renovation and new uses
  • Shared operational infrastructure — accounting, admin, property management, financing — built once and reused, so specialists (a chef, a shopkeeper, a farmer) can focus on their craft
  • A gradually assembled portfolio of complementary businesses that reinforce each other and the community

Success even if Sapana is the only business we ever own

Expansion is upside, not the bar for success. Sapana alone is a complete, worthwhile outcome if the restaurant becomes financially healthy, the owner and staff are able to remain in Japan, we genuinely enjoy operating it, and it contributes positively to the community. This decision needs to stand on its own merits, even in a future where Sapana is the only business we ever own.

Long-Term Flexibility

Owning a Japanese company shapes our own future optionality. Based on general principles of Japanese company law — not yet confirmed with a corporate lawyer or accountant, and treated here as a realistic overview rather than legal advice:

  • Continuing to own the company from abroad is likely straightforward. Foreign ownership of shares/membership interests generally doesn't require Japanese residency. If we eventually leave Japan, we could likely keep our ownership stake without issue.
  • Continuing to actively operate it without a Japanese visa is likely harder. Day-to-day management — banking, filings, a registered address — generally needs someone with legal authority physically able to act in Japan. May's Table 2 status gives her more flexibility here than a typical work visa would.
  • Local management could likely run the company on our behalf. A hired manager, an outsourced accounting/administrative service, or a trusted local partner could handle day-to-day compliance if neither of us were resident — a common setup, but one that adds cost and depends on trusting whoever holds that role.
  • The company can likely be kept dormant if priorities change. Japan has a formal dormant-company status for exactly this situation. Dormancy likely reduces, but doesn't eliminate, ongoing requirements.
  • Likely ongoing obligations even while dormant: annual tax filings even with zero activity, a registered representative with a Japan address, statutory bookkeeping retention, and possibly periodic filings to avoid administrative dissolution after extended inactivity.

None of this needs resolving before the August 6 meeting — it's here so the option space is visible before we commit, not to slow anything down. Confirm the specifics with a Japanese corporate lawyer or accountant before relying on any of it.

Immigration Summary

Current strategy: the owner and one additional staff member transition from Business Manager status (owner) to Skilled Labor (chef-specific) visas as New Roots Collective employees. May becomes Representative Director; Corey remains a shareholder/silent investor while staying employed at Indeed.

Why this is the preferred path: it avoids the post-October-2025 Business Manager requirements entirely (¥30M capital, a staffing test, ~N2 Japanese, 3+ years management experience) since it doesn't depend on New Roots Collective being separately qualified as a Business Manager sponsor. It also fits cleanly with both founders' existing statuses — no visa changes needed for Corey or May, no risk to Corey's HSP sponsorship at Indeed.

Major benefits: no ¥30M capitalization needed; a real path for the owner (and potentially other staff) to stay in Japan on solid legal footing; doesn't block the owner's future PR eligibility the way remaining on Business Manager status would.

Remaining risks: not yet confirmed by a licensed immigration specialist; unclear whether the owner's and staff member's actual backgrounds meet chef/skilled-labor skill-matching criteria.

Remaining unknowns: the owner's exact Business Manager renewal date; the additional staff member's real role and current visa category; whether a shareholder-only role fully protects Corey's HSP status.

Financial Summary

Current assumptions: the business is roughly break-even on an estimated ~¥1M/month in revenue — but whether "break-even" is calculated before or after owner compensation is unknown, and changes the read on everything else here.

Capital strategy: fund via personal savings, not debt. Comfortable up to ¥5M, possible up to ¥10M+ if justified by diligence; current soft target ¥2–5M. Reconsider the deal if the business is losing roughly ¥1M/month with no improvement, sustained for several months.

Why this approach: avoids compounding an unproven, first-time operating business with debt risk, and matches both current estimates and the immigration path above — which shouldn't require New Roots Collective to independently meet Business Manager-level capitalization.

Major upside: acquiring an already-operating, revenue-generating business rather than starting from zero.

Major risks: every number above is an unverified placeholder; entity type, corporate structure, acquisition structure, and purchase price approach are all still undecided pending real financials.

Major unknowns: the restaurant's real financials, a fair purchase price, and whether a direct-ownership or holding-company structure fits better long-term.

Local Trust

A long-term consideration to keep in view, not a primary factor in this specific decision.

Trust in a small rural community is earned over years, not granted on arrival. To some people, our first business may initially look like foreigners buying another foreign-owned business, regardless of intentions — that perception won't disappear just because we mean well or already have good relationships. Actions will matter more than announcements: preserving what people already love about the restaurant, and respecting local norms, will do more for trust than anything we say. Our existing relationships and the Minobu advisor's support reduce this challenge; they don't remove it. This isn't a reason to hesitate — it's a reason to expect the work of earning full local trust to continue well past whatever happens with this specific acquisition.
